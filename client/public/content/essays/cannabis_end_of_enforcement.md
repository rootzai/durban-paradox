# The Geometry of Smoke: Why the Law Must Eventually Inhale
### By M.P. Glassworthy, 9th Earl of Glassworthy
### With Representative Lou Correa (D-CA)

In the year of our Lord 2016, the carriage-masters of London did not merely attempt to regulate the horseless Uber; they sought to banish it to the outer darkness.

Transport for London, a guild of well-meaning bureaucrats who likely measure their tea in linear teaspoons rather than recursive Eighths, declared the ride-hailing entity "not fit and proper." The decree was absolute. The parchment was stamped. The authority was undeniable.

And then, as my father Jonatin often warned, the map collided with the territory.

Tens of thousands of drivers simply... stopped. The great gears of the city ground to a halt. Commuters missed their appointments; lovers missed their trysts. What the regulators had mistaken for a simple licensing dispute was, in fact, a structural failure of the city's own circulatory system. Uber was not a loophole to be closed; it was a new vein that had already grown into the heart of the metropolis.

The license was revoked. Then restored. Then revoked again. Then restored again. A pendulum swinging in a vacuum. The statutes remained rigid, but the enforcement became fluid. It was a lesson in the physics of governance: when the demand is as durable as stone and the enforcement is as disruptive as a storm, the law does not vanish. It simply ceases to govern the outcome. Regulation replaces prohibition not because the King desires it, but because the physics demand it.

## The American Paradox

This same geometric inevitability now defines the American relationship with the cannabis plant.

The recent proclamation by President Trump to move marijuana from Schedule I to Schedule III is being hailed by the town criers as a legal breakthrough. But we—myself and the good Representative Correa, a man who has championed the MORE Act and fought for the rights of veterans to access this medicine—know better. It is not a breakthrough. It is a concession. It is the moment the Crown acknowledges that the castle walls have been breached not by an army, but by the sheer, stubborn reality of the market.

The history of cannabis in these United States is not a story of rising tides. By every measure of the abacus, consumption has remained as stable as the North Star. Usage fluctuates with the whims of culture, yes, but it has never behaved like a plague requiring the constant escalation of force. What changed was not the amount of smoke in the air, but the amount of gold the government was willing to burn trying to catch it.

The Controlled Substances Act was built on a linear fallacy: that prohibition would suppress supply. Instead, it created a decentralized, rhizomatic market that adapted faster than any enforcement agency could print new forms. State legalization did not create the demand; it merely lit a candle in a room that was already full of people.

## The California Conundrum

Nowhere is this paradox more visible than in the Golden State, the home of my esteemed co-author. As Representative Correa has often argued in the halls of Congress, despite boasting the largest legal market in the realm, nearly 80% of transactions still occur in the shadows, outside the licensed system.

This is not a rebellion. It is an equation.

Legal operators face taxes that would make a medieval sheriff blush, licensing fees that rival the cost of a small armada, and compliance hurdles that require a team of scribes to navigate. The illicit sellers do not. Regulation exists on paper; the market exists in reality.

Schedule III does not solve this mismatch. It accelerates it.

By admitting that the plant has medical utility—a fact known to every village healer since the dawn of time—the reclassification weakens the moral foundation for aggressive enforcement without offering a scalable alternative. The prosecutors retain their swords, but their incentives dull. Resources shift. Enforcement becomes selective, like a gardener who only weeds the flower beds he can see from the porch.

## The Administrative Alignment

What follows is a pattern as old as bureaucracy itself. The law remains on the books, a dusty tome on a high shelf. But enforcement thins. Administrative discretion fills the gap. And over time, that discretion hardens into policy.

We have seen this geometry before. Ride-sharing prevailed because enforcing the taxi monopolies became too costly, both in coin and in political capital. Short-term rentals reshaped the zoning laws because too many homeowners were already participating. Gambling moved from the back alley to the tax roll once the state realized that policing the vice was less profitable than taxing the commerce.

In each case, the decisive question was not moral. It was logistical. When the rules no longer map to the reality, the reality prevails.

Cannabis has reached this inflection point. The current system of specialized dispensaries and segmented distribution is a transitional structure, a half-measure sustained by inertia. It is a bridge built of wood in an age of steel.

## The Quiet Conclusion

As the logic of enforcement gives way to the logic of regulation, control moves downstream. The government stops trying to burn the field and starts trying to manage the harvest. Age verification, labeling, taxation, and point-of-sale accountability replace the raids and the arrests.

Schedule III is not the destination. It is the fulcrum. It is the point where the mismatch between the statute and the enforcement becomes unsustainable. Descheduling will arrive not with a bang, but with a whisper. It will come as administrative alignment—the law finally catching up to a reality that enforcement conceded years ago.

The plant did not overwhelm the system. The system exhausted itself trying to govern a stable product with tools designed for a crisis.

These transitions rarely end with grand proclamations from the balcony. They end quietly, in the back rooms of the bureaucracy. They end with licenses, receipts, and tax forms. The war concludes not with a treaty, but with normalization.

The product is regulated where it matters most: at the register. And the smoke, finally, is allowed to rise.
