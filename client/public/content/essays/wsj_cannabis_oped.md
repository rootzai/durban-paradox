# The Geometry of the Black Market: Why Cannabis Enforcement Failed
### By Rep. Lou Correa and Eric Spitz

In 2016, London regulators attempted to ban Uber, declaring the ride-hailing service "not fit and proper." It was a decisive legal maneuver that failed almost immediately. The reason was not legal, but structural: Uber had become infrastructure. When a service is woven into the daily lives of millions, the law does not disappear, but it ceases to govern the outcome. Regulation replaces prohibition not by preference, but by necessity.

This same dynamic now defines American cannabis policy.

President Trump’s recent move to reclassify marijuana from Schedule I to Schedule III is being framed as a political breakthrough. In reality, it is an enforcement concession—an acknowledgment that the federal government has lost the ability to control the market through prohibition. The "war on drugs" did not end with a treaty; it ended with a geometric impossibility.

### The Linear Fallacy

For fifty years, the Controlled Substances Act operated on a linear fallacy: that interdiction would suppress supply, and suppressed supply would eliminate demand. This model assumed a centralized enemy—a cartel, a kingpin, a single point of failure.

But the cannabis market is not a pyramid; it is a rhizome—a decentralized, root-like structure that grows horizontally underground. It is adaptive, redundant, and deeply embedded in the American economy. By every credible measure, cannabis consumption has remained broadly stable for decades, fluctuating with culture rather than law enforcement. What changed was not the behavior of the American consumer, but the cost of trying to stop them.

We have seen this firsthand. One of us (Mr. Correa) has spent years in Congress fighting for the MORE Act and veterans' access to medical cannabis. The other (Mr. Spitz) co-founded C4 Distro with former California Attorney General Bill Lockyer, attempting to build a compliant supply chain in the world’s largest legal market.

Our combined experience points to a single, uncomfortable truth: The illicit market is not a rebellion. It is an efficiency engine.

### The California Paradox

Nowhere is this clearer than in California. Despite operating the largest legal cannabis market in the country, an estimated 80% of transactions still occur outside the licensed system.

This is not a failure of demand; it is a failure of geometry. The legal market is constructed with vertical walls: high taxes, restrictive zoning, and compliance costs that rival the defense industry. The illicit market is horizontal: it flows like water, finding the path of least resistance to the consumer. When the map (the law) conflicts with the territory (the market), the territory always wins.

When the government attempts to tax a commodity at 40% while offering no value add—no safety testing, no convenience, no branding that the consumer trusts more than their local dealer—the market corrects. It bypasses the tax.

Schedule III does not resolve this mismatch. By acknowledging the medical utility of cannabis, the federal government weakens the rationale for aggressive enforcement without offering a scalable alternative. Prosecutors retain the authority to raid, but the economic incentives to do so evaporate. Resources shift. Enforcement becomes selective.

### The Inevitability of Regulation

What follows is a pattern familiar to students of economic history. When enforcement becomes too costly—politically and fiscally—administrative discretion fills the gap.

We saw this with online gambling, which moved from the back alley to the tax roll once states realized that policing the demand was less profitable than taxing the commerce. We saw it with short-term rentals, which reshaped zoning laws simply by existing in too many neighborhoods to evict.

In each case, the decisive factor was logistical, not moral. When the rules no longer map to the reality, the reality prevails.

Cannabis has reached that inflection point. The current system of segmented state markets and cash-only banking is a transitional structure, sustained by inertia. It is a bridge built of wood in an age of steel.

### The Path Forward

The move to Schedule III is the fulcrum. It is the point where the mismatch between statute and enforcement becomes unsustainable. Descheduling will arrive not as a cultural reckoning, but as administrative alignment—the law finally catching up to a reality that enforcement conceded years earlier.

The goal now must be to manage the transition. We need a regulatory framework that acknowledges the geometry of the market. This means lower taxes to incentivize legal participation, interstate commerce to smooth supply shocks, and full banking access to ensure transparency.

The government stops trying to eliminate the product and begins managing how it is sold. Age verification, labeling, taxation, and point-of-sale accountability replace raids and arrests.

The cannabis market did not overwhelm the system. The system exhausted itself trying to govern a stable product with tools designed for a crisis. The war concludes not with a bang, but with a receipt.

*Mr. Correa, a Democrat, represents California’s 46th Congressional District. Mr. Spitz is the former CEO of C4 Distro and a longtime media executive.*
