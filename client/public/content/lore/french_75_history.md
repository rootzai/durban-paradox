# The History of the French 75: From Artillery to Aperitif

## Late 19th Century (1870s–1900): The Age of Industrial Power
This is where the story really begins. After the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71), Europe enters a long period of armed peace. Nations are not fighting each other constantly, but they are preparing obsessively. Industrial capacity becomes national identity. Railways, factories, ships, and weapons are no longer backstage infrastructure — they are symbols of modernity.

In this period, weapons are discussed the way we now discuss breakthrough technology. Newspapers cover new artillery designs. World’s fairs showcase military engineering alongside electricity and architecture. Military attachés trade intelligence openly. Civilians know the names of famous machines.

In 1897, France unveils the Canon de 75 modèle 1897. It is a sensation. Unlike previous artillery, it can fire rapidly and accurately without re-aiming after each shot. Engineers admire it. Strategists debate it. Journalists write about it. It becomes shorthand for speed, precision, and modern French ingenuity.

**Crucially:** At this moment, the gun is not yet associated with mass death. It is admired, even elegant.

## Belle Époque (1890s–1914): Elegance Meets Power
Europe — especially Paris — is at cultural peak confidence. This is the world of cafés, champagne, fashion, and wit. It is also a world deeply fascinated by machines. Cars, airplanes, and weapons are all part of the same mental category: fast, modern, dangerous, exciting.

Naming things after powerful machines is normal. It’s playful, ironic, and a little braggy. Somewhere in this era — likely just before or during the early years of WWI — a cocktail appears in Paris and London bars that mixes:
*   Gin (already associated with strength)
*   Lemon (sharpness)
*   Sugar (civilization)
*   Champagne (luxury)

It tastes light. It hits hard. Calling it the French 75 is a joke that instantly lands: Elegant on the surface. Shockingly powerful underneath. At this stage, the reference feels clever, not dark.

## World War I (1914–1918): The Metaphor Becomes Literal
When war breaks out, the French 75 is everywhere — for real. It becomes the backbone of French artillery and is used relentlessly. Soldiers nickname guns. Civilians read about them daily. The “75” stops being an abstract symbol and becomes an instrument of survival and slaughter.

The cultural meaning shifts:
*   **Before the war:** modern power, speed, prestige
*   **During the war:** relentless force, industrialized killing

But the name sticks — because humor and habit stick even as meaning darkens.

## Post-War (1919–1920s): Irony and Survival
After the war, Europe is traumatized, but nightlife explodes. People drink to forget, to celebrate survival, to feel normal again. Dark humor becomes common. Old metaphors don’t disappear — they are reinterpreted.

The French 75 now carries ironic weight: a drink named after a gun, consumed in peacetime, sparkling, elegant, lethal in its own small way. It survives because it captures the contradiction of the age better than almost anything else.

## Why It Feels Confusing Now
Today, weapons are politically charged rather than culturally shared, military technology is abstracted from daily civilian life, and we don’t casually reference artillery in jokes or menus. So the name sounds bizarre unless you reconstruct the world it came from. But in its original context, it made perfect sense.

## In One Sentence
The French 75 is called the French 75 because it was born in an era when industrial weapons were symbols of modern elegance and power, named after the most famous French artillery piece of its time, and preserved through a war that transformed that symbol from clever metaphor into dark irony. Once you see the timeline, the name stops being weird — and starts being disturbingly precise.
